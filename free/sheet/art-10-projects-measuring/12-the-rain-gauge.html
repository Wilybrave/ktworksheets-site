What you need: a plastic bottle · a jar · scissors · tape · a ruler · card · small stones · a pencil.
  1. A washing-up bowl and an egg cup are both left out in the same shower of rain. Which one ends up with deeper water in it?
Neither: the same. The bowl catches more, but spreads it over more floor, and the two cancel. So rain is measured as a depth, not an amount: half an inch in a thimble or a pool.
Test it. Put two straight-sided containers of very different widths out in the next shower, away from the house. Measure the depth in each.

Why a Bottle Lies

Making · Projects · Page 2 of 6

DIRECTIONS: Cut the top off the bottle and turn it upside down into the base. Then read this.
A bottle does not have straight sides. Its dimpled bottom and curved shoulder make a ruler read wrong at both ends.
The fix is to move the nought.

1. Cut the bottle across the shoulder. Turn the top upside down and drop it into the base — that is your funnel, and it is waterproof, which a card one would not be.

2. Drop a few small stones in the bottom so the wind cannot take it.

3. Pour water in until it is above the dimples and into the straight part, and the stones are covered.

4. Mark that line. That is nought, not the bottom of the bottle.

5. Every reading from now on is measured up from that mark, and you empty back down to it rather than emptying it out.
The page 3 strips tape on the outside, zero on your mark, on anything with a straight middle.

Making It Read Bigger

Making · Projects · Page 4 of 6

DIRECTIONS: Measure across the widest part of the funnel’s mouth, through the center, then the collector.
Catch it wide, collect it narrow, and the reading grows, which makes small rain easy to read. It grows by the SQUARE of the widths: a funnel twice as wide reads four times as much; three times as wide, nine.

Where It Goes

Making · Projects · Page 5 of 6

DIRECTIONS: Pick the spot before you start. Moving it later spoils the log.
Do thisBecause
Out in the open, well away from anything tallAt least twice as far away as the thing is high. A wall, a fence or a hedge makes a pocket of still air on one side and a gust on the other, and rain does not fall straight down through either.
Never under the eaves, a branch or a washing lineIt will read nothing at all in a light shower and then get a fortnight’s worth in one drip when the gutter overflows.
Rim about a foot off the groundLower and rain bouncing off the ground splashes back INTO it. Higher and the wind starts carrying drops over the top instead of into it.
Rim level, and check it again after a windy nightA tilted mouth is a smaller mouth as far as falling rain is concerned, so it under-reads — and it will not tell you it is doing it.
The same spot every timeThe whole value of a rain log is comparing one week with another. Move the gauge and you have started a new log.
Why twice the height away? Wind lifts over a wall and tumbles behind it, carrying rain to one side. Far enough away, it flattens out again.

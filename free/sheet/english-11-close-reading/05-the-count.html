The Count

The project was called Backyard Birds: A Two-Week Survey, and I had a clipboard for it, which my brother said was a lot of clipboard for a bird feeder.

The rules were simple. Every morning before school I sat at the kitchen window for fifteen minutes and wrote down every bird that landed on the feeder. Not the ones in the trees. Not the ones I thought I saw. Only the ones on the feeder, because Ms. Ortiz said a scientist counts what is there, not what she hopes is there. I wrote that inside the cover of my notebook in capital letters.

Grandpa had been living with us since March. He had the room that used to be the sewing room, and most of his things were still in boxes, because he said there was no hurry. He got up before anybody. When I came down on the first morning of the survey, he was already at the window with his coffee, and he had already written in my notebook.

Chickadee 3. Sparrow 5. Cardinal 2.

“You can’t do that,” I said. “It’s my project.”

“Just getting you started,” he said.

I crossed it out — neatly, one line, the way Ms. Ortiz showed us, so you can still read the mistake — and did my fifteen minutes. Two chickadees. Four sparrows. One cardinal, a male, so red he looked fake against the snow.

The next morning he had done it again. Cardinal 2. And the morning after that. His numbers were always close to mine, except for the cardinals. I always saw one. He always wrote two.

“There’s only one,” I told him on Thursday. I said it slowly, the way you say things to my brother. “I’ve been watching. It’s the same bird every time.”

Grandpa looked at the feeder for a long time. “Cardinals don’t go around alone,” he said. “Not as a rule.” Then he took his cup to the sink, and he took the other cup too, the one that was always on the windowsill next to his, still full, gone cold with a skin on top. I had thought it was for Mom. Mom drinks tea.

I started getting up earlier so I could get to the notebook first. It didn’t matter. He was always there before me, the notebook open at the right page and the pencil lying across it, and there it would be in his shaky up-and-down handwriting. Cardinal 2. So I went back to the first page and wrote a correction in the margin in red next to every one of his: 1 — observed. After that I did it every morning. By the second week the margin was a column of little red ones, all the way down the page, like a fence.

My brother said I was being mean. I said I was being accurate. Those are different things, and I am the one doing the project.

The Count

On the last morning of the survey I set my alarm for 5:30. The kitchen was dark and the window was gray, and Grandpa was in his chair with the two cups in front of him, the way he always was. He didn’t hear me come in. He was talking, very quietly, and not to me.

“There he is,” he said. “Look at that. Rosie, look at him.”

The cardinal was on the feeder. One cardinal. I stood in the doorway with my clipboard and I counted him, because that is what I was there to do. One.

Grandpa turned around then and saw me. He didn’t look embarrassed, which is how I would have looked. He just slid the notebook across the table so it was in front of my chair.

“All yours,” he said. “Your count.”

I sat down. He had already written today’s line. Cardinal 2. The red pencil was right there next to it, where I had left it the day before. I picked it up. The cardinal sat on the feeder being red, and Grandpa’s coffee was steaming, and the other cup wasn’t.

Ms. Ortiz says a scientist counts what is there.

I put the red pencil down.

I handed in the project on Monday. The table was right, and the graph, and the conclusion. If you turn to the last page you will see a column of red ones running down the margin, thirteen of them, and then a space at the bottom where the fourteenth should be. Ms. Ortiz circled the space and drew a question mark next to it.

I never answered it.

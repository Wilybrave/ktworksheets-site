Metal rings; wood thumps. Bending rubs a material against itself and turns movement into heat. Metal loses little; wood and plastic lose it at once. A crack stops the ring dead: that is how bells and train wheels are tested.

It Needs a Sail

Making · Projects · Page 4 of 7

DIRECTIONS: Cut one out: the wind catcher is what makes the chime play.
The flat thing hanging off the bottom of every wind chime is not decoration. It is the engine.

The clapper in the middle is heavy, and a breeze cannot shift it. So the sail hangs below on a longer string, where it has room to swing and a broad face for the wind to push on — and it drags the clapper into the tubes. Leave the sail off and your chime hangs silent on a day when the one three gardens away is going.
No sail. The wind goes past a thin clapper and nothing happens.
With a sail. Same breeze, same chime.

Building It, and the Rule That Is Not What You Expect

Making · Projects · Page 6 of 7

DIRECTIONS: Hang the longest first, then work round. Tie knots loosely at first.
StepHowWhy
1. The topA stick, or a metal jar lid with holes punched round the edge.It has to be stiff. A floppy top absorbs the ring, same as a bad knot.
2. Hang them in a ringAll the same distance from the middle, all hanging to the same height at the top.The clapper has to be able to reach every one of them. Miss one and it never plays.
3. The clapperSomething wooden, hanging in the middle, at the height of the middle of the tubes.Wood on metal is a note. Metal on metal is a clank. And the middle of the tube is where it moves most.
4. The sailBelow the clapper, on a longer string.Page 4. It is the engine.
5. Order themLongest to shortest, going round.The wind plays them in the order you hang them, so hanging them in order means it plays a tune rather than a jumble.
Halving a string doubles the pitch. Halving a bar makes it four times higher: two octaves. So one short tube pings out above the rest. Try a long fork beside a teaspoon.
